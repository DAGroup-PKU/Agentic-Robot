1. Summary

General-purpose agents can develop and execute manipulation tasks in the real world. They can write control programs, analyze physical feedback, revise manipulation strategies, and remain involved during execution to guide the next action. The capabilities and costs required to complete a task, however, vary with the task, implementation, and available human support.

We studied Astra and Fable 5.1 on three tasks using a dual-arm robot platform: block stacking, disc stacking, and Ethernet insertion. Both models completed the two stacking tasks in the recorded rollouts, while their Ethernet insertion results differed substantially. Reaching the first successful solution sooner did not necessarily lead to faster repeated execution.

Three observations stand out. Online model participation can help resolve local manipulation decisions. Program-driven execution can reduce the cost of repeated runs. Exploration direction, experience continuity, and some physical information still require human support. Understanding an agent’s performance in the real world requires examining what it achieves, how it achieves it, and the time and human effort involved.

2. Introduction

Controlling real robots through software tools requires handling calibration errors, changing contact conditions, sensor biases, and execution delays. Recovering from a failed attempt also carries a practical cost.

We investigated five questions about general-purpose agents under these conditions:

  • Which manipulation tasks can they complete in the real world?
  • How do they explore and develop working solutions?
  • How efficiently do those solutions execute in repeated runs?
  • What role does human support play in exploration and execution?
  • How do session changes and stored experience affect subsequent performance?

Our study therefore examines two stages: developing a working solution and executing it repeatedly. Task completion provides the outcome; exploration records, implementations, and human interactions help explain it.

3. System and Evaluation Setup

Hardware platform

The platform consists of two Franka arms, two Robotiq grippers, two wrist-mounted RealSense D405 cameras, and one ZED Mini head camera. The tasks cover basic pick-and-place, multistage disc assembly, and Ethernet insertion requiring fine alignment and contact handling.

Harness: Engineer, Runner, and human support

Our workflow uses two roles. The Engineer organizes task goals, stage plans, and result evaluation. The Runner writes and executes code, returning physical feedback for the next iteration. Humans provide calibration, interpretations of the scene, or methodological suggestions when needed.

Agents choose their own implementation approach. They may encode more of the task in a program or continue observing, reasoning, and adjusting during execution. The resulting implementation is itself part of what we study.

Exploration and execution

During exploration, we record the time from the start of exploration to the first successful completion, together with the human assistance provided. During execution, we record task success rate, total execution time, agent online time, and the number of agent activations. These measurements distinguish the effort required to develop a solution from the efficiency and degree of model involvement required to run it.

The current results page presents 30 selected recordings, with five runs per model per task. Development budgets, initial conditions, and human support were not strictly matched, so the results describe the recorded implementations. Exploration durations are operator-reported and include some waiting time. Ethernet success means mechanical insertion; electrical connectivity has not been verified.

4. Evaluation Results

Task completion: performance differs across tasks

In the selected recordings, both Astra and Fable 5.1 completed five disc-stacking runs and five block-stacking runs. For Ethernet insertion, Astra completed five mechanical insertions, while none of Fable 5.1’s five runs completed the task.

Completing basic grasping and transport does not automatically resolve the final alignment and contact problem. A local bottleneck can determine whether the entire implementation succeeds. Results and recordings are available on the project website.

Exploration: agent style influences time to first success

Astra tends to remain involved during execution, advancing the task through observation, judgment, and adjustment. This allows it to complete a task through online participation before a fully automated program is mature. Fable tends to implement the task as a program, repeatedly updating and optimizing code until it succeeds, often requiring more development effort upfront.

This difference was particularly apparent in Ethernet insertion and disc stacking. Astra reached first success earlier in disc stacking and achieved Ethernet insertion, which Fable did not complete during the reported exploration period. Fable completed development of the block-stacking task sooner.

Execution: online participation and program-driven execution have different costs

Fable 5.1’s recorded executions were faster on both stacking tasks. Disc stacking averaged approximately 7.3 minutes for Fable and 46.5 minutes for Astra. Block stacking averaged approximately 1.1 and 5.8 minutes, respectively.

This difference is also associated with execution style. Astra retains more decisions in the execution loop, adding online participation through repeated observation and adjustment. Fable concentrates more work in development; once its program is ready, it can advance continuously with less dependence on the agent.

Time must also be interpreted alongside the outcome. An insertion run that stops earlier has not necessarily completed the task more efficiently.

Task-level implementations and failure analysis

Astra generally advances through short, checked actions, reviewing feedback between stages and revising the next move. Fable 5.1 more often combines perception, geometry, control, and retries into a continuously running program.

Both approaches can use feedback. The main distinction is which feedback the program handles and which feedback the model must interpret again. This division affects task completion, runtime, and exception handling.

5. Findings

Online model participation can address local states that a program does not yet capture

Ethernet insertion illustrates the value of online judgment. Astra reads raw images and specifies a movement direction and distance. A controller executes the command, a script checks insertion, and the model observes again if needed. Fable 5.1 explored visual alignment and admittance control, but its recorded implementation did not complete insertion. These observations support further study of hybrid execution; isolating the contribution of online participation requires additional controlled comparisons.

First success and efficient repeated execution are different objectives

Keeping the model involved reduces the need to encode every situation in advance, but repeated observation, reasoning, and confirmation add time. The advantage in reaching first success varies across tasks, and reaching a working solution sooner does not guarantee lower repeated execution costs.

Calibration, verification, and recovery affect exploration efficiency

Astra is more sensitive to calibration discrepancies and spends more time checking them. That caution can prevent errors, but it can also prolong a task that was already feasible. Verification and recovery require a similar balance: checks should reflect actual task requirements and allow reobservation or a local retry when evidence is insufficient.

Agents can improve local manipulation strategies through failure feedback

Agents can define evaluation criteria before an attempt and modify their approach afterward. Changes extend beyond parameters to poses and action sequences. For example, after vertical disc handling caused interference, an agent adopted a tilted approach, turning physical feedback into a concrete change in the next attempt.

Local improvement does not guarantee effective management of exploration

Agents can spend a long time optimizing one method while rarely comparing alternatives proactively. They may abandon a method after prolonged lack of progress, but timely switching usually requires a human request. Diagnosing failure, improving the current method, and deciding whether that method remains worth pursuing are distinct capabilities.

Humans provide information and influence how agents explore

Human help supplies physical information, interprets observations, and redirects exploration. After a human calibrated the fixed post position, for example, Fable could handle discs placed randomly within the workspace.

Expectations of help also affect behavior. When grasping clearance was insufficient, Fable initially asked a human to move the disc. After being told that no assistance would be provided, it closed the gripper and pushed the disc into a more accessible position. The content and timing of assistance are more informative than a binary label of human involvement.

6. Discussion and Open Questions

Assign responsibilities according to the nature of the decision

High-frequency motion, explicit constraints, and mature local control are well suited to programs. Models are better placed where a decision requires interpreting observations, diagnosing an exception, or changing an approach. As the relevant conditions and methods become clearer, stable decisions can be captured in reusable code.

How should experience be preserved across sessions?

Some of a successful solution resides in code; other parts may reside in the current conversation, experience documents, and implicit operating conditions. A fresh session may need further attempts to recover previous performance, even with earlier records. Conversely, restarting can sometimes improve the speed of a long-running session.

Which information belongs in active context, which should become code or external experience, and which is outdated? We need to evaluate how preserving experience affects completion, recovery, and execution time to establish a reliable handoff process.

How should efficiency and precision be balanced?

More observations, smaller movements, and additional checks usually cost time, without always producing a corresponding benefit. Error tolerance also changes across a task: an offset that is acceptable during transport may determine success during final insertion.

A next step is to investigate whether the system can allocate observation, reasoning, and control effort according to task stage and uncertainty. This requires measuring completion, execution time, and human effort under the same success criteria to establish whether an approach improves real-world working efficiency.
